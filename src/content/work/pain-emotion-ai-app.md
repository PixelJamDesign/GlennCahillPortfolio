    answer: >-
      Handwritten notes are hard to transfer between GPs, hard to read, and hard
      to interpret months later, so the narrative of what a patient was actually
      going through rarely survived the journey. Chronic pain is longitudinal by
      nature, but the record of it was episodic. The challenge was to build a
      patient profile that tells the story, so care is properly informed from
      day one.

      led to the core principle - the patient's own words are the richest data

  - chapter: The work
    question: What was my approach?
    show: true
    answer: >-
      I started with the consultation itself, because that's where the richest
      information already exists - it just wasn't being captured. The app
      records and transcribes the conversation live, then matches keywords
      against Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions and colours them accordingly. Words
      like excruciating, niggling, infuriating carry real emotional weight, and
      I wanted the interface to surface that language rather than flatten it
      into a number out of ten.
  - chapter: The work
    question: What did I design?
    show: true
    answer: >-
      The full iPad experience. A three dee mannequin for marking pain
      locations, the live transcript with inline emotion colouring, a circular
      pain time map that places pain across the day, and supporting widgets for
      weekly steps, weight against target and sleep, drawn from devices patients
      already wear. I also designed a Plutchik wheel visualisation that renders
      a whole session as a single emotional footprint, with date navigation
      between visits and a scrubbable timeline to watch that shape change over
      months of care.
  - chapter: The work
    question: What were the key considerations?
    show: true
    answer: >-
      Three things. The mannequin is deliberately generic - a diagram, not a
      mirror. It's a clinical tool, and it shouldn't invite a patient to read
      anything about their own body into it. Second, the AI is a first pass and
      never the authority. Every tagged word has a dropdown so the clinician can
      reassign the emotion, any word can be long pressed to add context the AI
      missed, and the recording stays available so notes can be refined
      afterwards. Third, vagueness is treated as useful. When a patient says
      afternoon, that ambiguity is visible on the map, and it prompts a better
      question.
  - chapter: The result
    question: What was the outcome?
    show: true
    answer: >-
      The client's reaction was extremely positive. What stopped it was timing
      rather than appetite: AI in twenty eighteen simply wasn't mature enough to
      make live emotional analysis of a clinical conversation reliable enough to
      take to market. It remained a concept piece, but one that proved its point
      - that a patient's own language is a viable clinical signal, and that it
      can be visualised in a way a doctor can act on.

## Background

Chronic pain isn't resolved in a fortnight. It's managed over months and years, across many appointments and often more than one practitioner, yet the record of it was frequently pen and paper. The opportunity was to give clinicians a way to capture not just where a patient hurts, but how they feel about it, and to let that picture build over time.

![](/images/work/pain-emotion-ai-app/01.webp)

## The work

I led the concept, art direction and prototyping, with my team lead giving me room to run with it. The result was a working prototype that took a recorded consultation and turned it into a visual, emotional record of a patient's pain.

![](/images/work/pain-emotion-ai-app/02.webp)

## The result

The concept was presented to the client, who were genuinely taken with it. In the end the technology of the day couldn't carry it - but the thinking has aged rather well.
